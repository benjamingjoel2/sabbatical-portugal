A season in Portugal is different from a holiday. You are not trying to see everything in ten days. You are trying to build a small, real life somewhere new: a home that works, a café that knows your order, a few friends you will keep. That takes a bit more planning up front, and a lot less rushing once you arrive.

This is the timeline we use with our own guests.

## Start with how long, not where

Most people start by choosing a place. We suggest starting with time, because the length of your stay decides almost everything else, especially the paperwork.

- **Up to 90 days.** If you hold a passport that is visa-exempt for the Schengen area, you can usually stay up to 90 days in any 180-day period without a visa. That limit covers the whole Schengen area, not just Portugal, and recent trips count towards it.
- **Three to twelve months.** Anything longer than 90 days needs a national visa, applied for before you travel. For most remote workers that means the D8. For people living on pensions or other passive income, it is usually the D7.
- **EU, EEA and Swiss citizens** can stay as long as they like, and simply register their residence after three months.

If you are not sure which group you fall into, or your situation is unusual, ask before you book anything. Visa applications for our guests are handled by partner immigration lawyers, and a short conversation early on can save months later.

## Then choose a place that fits your days

Portugal is small, but life looks very different from one region to the next. Think about an ordinary Tuesday, not a perfect Saturday.

- **Cities** (Lisbon, Porto, Coimbra): walkable neighborhoods, culture, easy social life, no car needed.
- **Coast** (Cascais, Ericeira, Comporta, the Algarve): ocean rhythm, outdoor life, quieter evenings, a car often helps.
- **Countryside** (the Alentejo, the Douro): space, silence, wine and long lunches, with a car essential.
- **Islands** (Madeira, the Azores): mild weather, dramatic nature and strong small communities.

A few honest questions help. How much do you want to drive? Do you need a big community or are a few good neighbors enough? Do you prefer heat or green? Do you mind rain in winter? Porto and the Azores are wonderful, but they are not dry.

Many guests split a longer stay, for example three months in Lisbon followed by three in Madeira. That works well and lets you see more of the country without feeling like a tourist.

## Six to nine months before: the big decisions

This is the planning window for longer stays and for families.

1. **Fix your dates and budget.** Be realistic. Rent is the biggest cost, and it varies a lot: a one-bedroom in central Lisbon costs far more than one in Coimbra or Évora.
2. **Start your visa conversation** if you are staying more than 90 days. Consular appointments can take time, and documents such as criminal record certificates often need apostilles and translations.
3. **Shortlist schools** if you have children. Popular international schools can fill early.
4. **Think about tax.** Spending a long time in Portugal can affect where you are tax resident. Talk to a qualified tax adviser in both countries before you commit. We do not give tax advice, but we can introduce you to people who do.

## Three months before: the home

For short stays, a furnished monthly rental is usually best. For six months or more, a longer lease is often cheaper and is commonly needed for residence applications.

Whatever you choose, look for:

- **A proper workspace.** A real desk and chair, not the kitchen table.
- **Tested internet.** Ask for a speed test, not a promise. Fibre is common in towns. Rural and island homes may rely on 4G or 5G routers.
- **Heating.** Portuguese homes can be cold and damp in winter. Ask what heating there is, and whether there is a dehumidifier.
- **Location over size.** A smaller home near a market, a café and a bus stop beats a larger one you have to drive from.

## One month before: the small things

These are the details that make the first week easy.

- Arrange travel and health insurance that covers your whole stay.
- Get a NIF, your Portuguese tax number, if you will need one for a lease, utilities or a bank account.
- Order a local SIM or eSIM so you have data at the airport.
- Check how your driving licence works in Portugal if you plan to drive.
- Make copies of key documents and store them somewhere secure.

## The first two weeks: settle on purpose

The temptation is to explore everything at once. Instead, build a routine early.

- **Walk your neighborhood** in the first few days. Find the market, pharmacy, bakery and a café you like.
- **Choose your working rhythm.** Try a coworking space at least once, even if you prefer working from home. It is the easiest place to meet people.
- **Book a language class.** Even a few phrases change how people respond to you.
- **Say yes to the first invitation.** A community dinner in your first week is worth more than any guidebook.

## The middle months: go a little further

Once the basics feel normal, use your weekends. A night in the Douro, or a weekend in the Alentejo. Portugal rewards slow travel, and most of it is within a few hours by train or car.

This is also the time to check in with yourself. Are you working too much? Too little? Is the place still right? It is completely fine to change destination halfway through a season. Many people do.

## Before you leave: close well

- Give notice on your lease in good time and agree how the deposit will be returned.
- Cancel utilities and subscriptions that are in your name.
- If you were here under short-stay rules, double-check your days.
- Say proper goodbyes. Many of our guests come back, and the people you met are the reason.

## A simple rule

Plan the paperwork early, choose your place for ordinary days, and leave space for the unexpected. That is most of the secret. The rest is long lunches, good light and letting a new place become yours for a while.
